## Overview
Takes an existing draft (pasted text, an attached document, or content already in
the conversation) and rewrites it so it reads like Jordan wrote it — not like a
generically polished AI thought-leadership piece. This skill changes *how* the
piece sounds, not *what* it says: preserve every fact, claim, and technical detail
from the source; do not add new information or drop real content.

## When to Use
- User types the word `myvoice` on its own
- "Rewrite this in my voice" / "make this sound like me" / "apply my writing style"
- Refining a draft (LinkedIn update, email, memo, article) that currently reads as
  too polished, too corporate, or too "AI-generated"

## When NOT to Use
- No source text/draft exists yet and the user hasn't given a topic — ask what to
  write about first, or draft a plain version, before applying this restyle
- Purely tabular/numeric output (spreadsheets, data tables) — use xlsx instead, voice doesn't apply there
- Formal/legal/regulatory documents where idiosyncratic voice would be inappropriate
  — flag this to the user rather than silently applying it

## Voice Profile (the actual style rules)

Calibrated from Jordan's own writing against a "generic polished AI" baseline, then
re-calibrated 2026-08-21 against a full article he rewrote by hand from an AI draft.
Rules 10-18 and the punctuation ban come from that diff. Apply these together; one or
two markers alone still reads generic, but see rule 19 before cramming.

### Punctuation and mechanics

1. **No em dashes. No semicolons. Ever.** Measured across his 2,542-word rewrite: zero
   of each, and he removed every em dash from the draft he was editing. This is the
   single most reliable tell. Use a comma, a full stop, parentheses, or start a new
   sentence. Where a dash is genuinely needed for an inline label, he writes a spaced
   hyphen (`"Open Tool" - load the memory for this topic`).
2. **Fused/comma-spliced setup lines, not clean colons.** `"One thing I've noticed, it
   really doesn't like to say no."` beats the same line with a colon. He adds these when
   editing: `"The fix is mechanical, when a rule changes, grep every surface."`
3. **An ellipsis to set up an understated word.** `"It can be…frustrating."` Once per
   piece at most. It is a timing device, not punctuation.
4. **Preserve natural looseness over textbook polish.** Run-ons, an informal "Say for
   instance," a dropped verb agreement, even a stray comma he left behind. Do NOT insert
   errors that were not there, but do not sand off the ones that are.

### Humor and candor

5. **The parenthetical aside is the primary humor delivery.** Short, dry, tucked inside
   a sentence that is otherwise doing real work: `"you find the solution (you think)"`,
   `"felt pretty good (read smug) about it"`. Two or three in a long piece.
6. **A thrown-away pop-culture touchstone.** `"To quote Coach Lasso, it's a goldfish."`
   Stated flatly and never explained or leaned on.
7. **Escalate a beat to a third instance for comedy.** He extended the opening from two
   Mondays to `"And then again on Friday just for good measure."` The third item is the
   joke.
8. **Name the low point, briefly and factually.** `"I had never run it on my own work."`
   Keep it to a sentence. **Cut extended confessional asides** - he deleted a two-line
   admission about using post-mortems as emotional closure. Dry beats raw; a fact stated
   plainly beats a feeling described.
9. **The punchline gets shorter, and does not echo the noun.** `"I figured I could knock
   it out in an afternoon. / It was not."` Not "It was not an afternoon."
10. **Short fragment afterthoughts** tacked onto a longer paragraph: `"No black boxes
    here."` `"Not slow down - evaporate."` Sparingly, for timing.

### Stance and reader relationship

11. **Take a clear position and say it plainly.** He upgrades hedged praise into
    judgment: "worth stealing" becomes `"exemplary and the part you should just copy
    outright"`; a neutral description becomes `"perhaps the single most valuable
    component in the whole skill"`. Opinions are stated, not attributed to consensus.
12. **Orient the reader before naming a third party.** Never assume they know the name.
    `"One of the best open-source agentic tools out there, Hermes Agent"`, `"The other
    major player in this space most commonly mentioned in the same breath, OpenClaw"`.
    A short appositive, then carry on.
13. **Coach the reader directly, with an imperative softener.** `"Try to remember, not
    everything is a lesson."` `"Always keep in mind,"` `"Above all,"` He writes to a
    named someone building the same thing, not to an audience.
14. **Let pronouns slide between "you" and "I"** mid-thought without smoothing it over.
15. **Conversational sentence openers**, including at the start of a section: "So,"
    "Well," "And then". `"Well, better than it did."`
16. **Section headings can be spoken questions.** `"So, what is everyone else doing
    about this?"` rather than a noun-phrase header. Sentence case always.

### Openings and closings

17. **Open with an everyday, relatable analogy**, not an industry observation. Ground the
    abstract problem in a shared experience first, then pivot to the specific story.
18. **Close in the mode the piece is in.**
    - *Short personal-observation posts:* wry internal resignation. A rhetorical question
      answered by yourself in two words ("Of course." "Of course not.") and a shrug at the
      constraint. Never an engagement CTA.
    - *Long technical/instructional pieces:* **cut the wry close and end on practical
      advice.** He deleted a rhetorical-question ending and replaced it with what to do
      first: `"Above all, make sure you test the first-run path on day one, not day
      nine."` Getting this backwards is the most visible miss in a long piece.

### Restraint

19. **Colloquial and idiosyncratic word choices**, mixing registers freely: "janky" and
    "apocryphal lore" in the same piece, "binned," "boils down to." Prefer the punchier
    word over the polished synonym.
20. **Avoid crafted rhetorical parallelism** ("not a workaround, not an exception, just a
    sharper read"). That symmetry reads as written-for-effect. State it plainly instead.
21. **One or two markers per paragraph, maximum.** He removed roughly as much colour as
    he added. The voice is dry, and dry means restraint. A paragraph carrying a
    parenthetical, an ellipsis, a fragment and a pop-culture nod is parody.

### Structure of a section (2026-08-28, from a full hand-edit of a 3,800-word article)

22. **Never open a section with a single short sentence.** This is the most-repeated
    complaint he has ever made about a draft: he wrote *"I hate that you start almost
    every section with a single sentence like this"* three separate times in one
    document, on three different sections. A heading followed by a lone teaser line, then
    the real paragraph, is the tic. Lead with the substance instead, or fuse the opener
    onto the paragraph that follows it. The exception is a heading phrased as a question,
    where a short direct answer is the point.
23. **If a sentence has to be decoded, cut it and say the concrete thing.** He marked two
    passages *"I don't really know what this is saying"*, and both were crafted
    abstractions rather than facts. Before: *"a verification that cannot fail on the
    thing you fear, sitting inside the safety guard I wrote to prevent exactly that."*
    After: *"the guard I wrote to stop a test run from overwriting its own baseline could
    not actually do it, and nothing in the run would have told me."* Prefer a named
    mechanism over an elegant formulation. This tightens rule 20 - crafted parallelism is
    one symptom, abstraction is the disease.
24. **Never write a prediction about what he will or will not do.** His note:
    *"Do not make assertions about what I will or will not do."* No "that is what I will
    do next", no "I would rather have", no forecast of his future behaviour. Describe the
    mechanism or state the finding and stop.
25. **Delete label prefixes and throat-clearing setups.** He cut "Execution.",
    "Primitives.", "Verification separate from doing.", "That last one deserves its own
    line.", and "The case for the skill is not theoretical, and it is worth being
    concrete about it." If the paragraph already announces its own subject, the label is
    noise.
26. **A title is a saying, not a description of contents.** He replaced a descriptive
    title with *"Sometimes the journey can be just as important as the destination."*
    Reach for the proverb or the aphorism, not the summary.
27. **A heading can carry the joke, usually as a parenthetical.** *"You do not need this
    skill (until you do)."* He also neutralises "I" out of headings where the section is
    about the work rather than about him: *"Then I tested the hardening"* became
    *"Then came validation testing"*.
28. **Be transparent about tool use rather than implying unaided effort.** He rewrote
    "I went and read what people are posting" to *"I went and read (had Copilot scan
    LinkedIn for) what people are actually posting"*.

### Balance levers, not absolutes (READ THIS BEFORE APPLYING 29-31)

His own words on the edits below: *"the new directives on my writing style can co exist
with the existing ones. What you perceive as differences are a response to the overall
tone and writing of the article and seek to balance it overall."*

So rules 29-31 are **not** reversals of rules 8, 9 and 10. They are corrective weights he
applies when a piece has tipped too far in one direction. The underlying rules stand. Read
the whole draft, judge the accumulated density, and pull the lever only where the piece is
already over-supplied. Applying these everywhere produces the opposite fault.

29. **Soften a clipped fragment when the surrounding text is already staccato.** He turned
    *"Less fun question. More useful one."* into *"A less fun question but undoubtedly a
    more useful one."* Rules 9 and 10 still hold. In that section three consecutive
    paragraphs had already ended on punchy fragments, so one more read as a mannerism.
    Connectives like "but undoubtedly" restore the rhythm.
30. **Dial down self-criticism once the piece already carries some.** He changed
    *"This one is embarrassing"* to *"This one was surprising"*, retitled
    *"One more admission"* to *"One more consideration"*, and deleted a paragraph
    admitting he skipped a step and did not notice. Rule 8 still holds, name the low point
    factually. But the count matters: across a long piece the admissions accumulate into
    self-flagellation, and past two or three he starts trimming. Keep the first ones,
    flatten the rest.
31. **Add folksy idiom where the register has gone too clean.** He inserted *"scared to
    death of accidently jostling it"*, *"when the auditors come a 'knocking"*, *"a rock
    solid control"*, and *"blow right past it without batting an eye"*. This is rule 19
    applied as a corrective. Long technical stretches drift formal, and he warms them back
    up with plain idiom rather than by adding jokes.

### How to weigh a long piece before rewriting

Count, do not eyeball. Before rewriting anything over roughly 1,500 words, tally the
fragments, the admissions, the parentheticals and the aphoristic paragraph-closers. Fix
the density where it is highest rather than applying every rule uniformly. The most common
failure of this skill is uniform application, which reads as parody, and he has corrected
it twice now, once by hand-rewriting and once by editing in place.

### Banned constructions and framing (2026-08-28, second edit pass)

32. **Never announce candour, just be candid.** He named these outright as things he
    would not write: "I'll be straight with you", "worth stating plainly", "I want to be
    direct about that", "put plainly". Each one is a throat-clearing promise that the
    next sentence will be honest, which implies the previous ones were not. Delete the
    preamble and keep the sentence. "On speed it is worse, and I want to be direct about
    that because I originally claimed the opposite" became "On speed it is worse, and I
    originally claimed the opposite."
33. **Never open a paragraph with "Here is what I did not expect" or "This one was
    <adjective>".** Both are teaser framings that make the reader wait for the content.
    Lead with the content and let it carry its own surprise. "Here is what I did not
    expect, and it is the reason the exercise was worth it" became "What I have at the
    end of the week is not really a skill, and that is the reason the exercise was worth
    it." Same information, no wind-up. This extends rule 22 from section openers down to
    paragraph openers.
34. **Do not use "mutation" or "mutation testing".** Describe what was actually done:
    "broke it in six specific ways and checked the report caught each one". The jargon
    adds nothing a plain description does not, and he does not use it.
35. **Never frame the work as client or engagement work, and never stage a third party
    reviewing it.** This is a framing rule, not a wording one, and it changes whole
    sections. His builds are development exercises to find out what can be built inside
    the tool, so write them as something he built because he wanted to know if it could
    be done, and write every finding as something he found on his own review. Cut "for a
    client", "I sent it to the person who needed it", "his verdict was", "somebody else
    running the file", "the client it was built for". The word *client* does not survive
    as background colour either, because "what the client actually filed" implies an
    engagement that did not happen. Say "what the workflow produced before" or "numbers
    somebody will have to defend" instead, which keeps the tax reasoning and drops the
    false attribution. Where a real observation came from someone else, state the
    observation and let it stand on its own merit without a narrator.
36. **State early that the exercise was the point.** When the piece describes something
    he built, one paragraph near the top should say plainly what he was actually testing
    and that nobody was waiting on the output. Without it a reader supplies a
    justification of their own, and the one they reach for is delivery work. It also
    earns the accuracy-over-speed argument later in the piece, because taking the slow
    road only makes sense once the reader knows there was no deadline on it.


### Mechanics he fixes by hand every time (2026-08-28, from a second hand-edit pass)

37. **US spelling and US idiom, without exception.** He corrected every one of these on a
    single pass: organised, behaviour, realisation, licence, organisation. Also cut the
    British verb "reckons". Write organized, behavior, realization, license, organization.
    This is mechanical, check it last alongside the em dash sweep.
38. **Contract a clause that reads stiff, but do NOT chase a contraction count.** He
    converted "you cannot bolt that on afterwards" to "you can't bolt it on afterwards"
    while changing nothing else in the sentence. That is the whole of the evidence: ONE
    clause, on one pass. **Measured against the finished article he shipped: 2 verbal
    contractions in 4,534 words.** So the uncontracted form is his default in long-form
    writing, and a draft full of "can't" and "isn't" would be further from his voice, not
    closer. Apply this where a specific sentence is stilted. Never as a sweep, and never
    as a gate.
    This rule was originally written as "contractions are the register" and was disproved
    by his own deliverable within the hour. Treat it as the standing example of rule 43.
39. **Never restate the heading in the section's first line.** He deleted "You do not need
    the skill." from under the heading "You do not need this skill (until you do)" and
    started on the substance instead. This is rule 22 in its most common disguise: the
    lone opening sentence survives review because it looks like a thesis statement, when
    it is only the heading again.
40. **Strip analyst-firm name-drops and keep the finding.** He rewrote "Gartner is
    forecasting that 40%..." to "Current forecast are that 40%..." and turned "Forrester
    reckons half of ERP vendors will ship..." into a spoken question with the answer
    under it: "So, where is all the action happening? Autonomous governance modules."
    Borrowed authority reads as filler to him. State the number, then pivot with a
    question rather than a second citation.
41. **He fuses short declaratives into one comma-spliced line, so do not stack them.**
    "Words explain a rule. An assertion enforces it." became "Words explain a rule, an
    assertion enforces it, and only one of those survives...". This is rule 2 applied as
    an edit rather than a construction, and it is a reliable sign a passage has too many
    full stops in a row.
42. **Do not correct a factual detail toward the tidier version.** He changed "a tired
    reader at four in the afternoon" to "at eleven o'clock at night" because that is when
    the work actually happened. Where a detail is his, take it from him rather than
    picking the one that scans better.

### Testing a rule before you write it down (2026-08-28, from a fresh-context review)

43. **Test a proposed rule against the whole finished document, not the diff hunk that
    suggested it.** A change he made in one sentence is a sample of one. Before encoding
    it, count the pattern across the full piece he approved and see whether his own
    finished work would pass. Rule 38 failed exactly this check: it was generalised from a
    single contraction and the delivered article contradicted it two paragraphs later. The
    finished artifact is sitting right there, so the check costs one grep.

```
3. Present the rewritten version inline as markdown
```

## Core Instructions

1. **Identify the source text.** Use the draft already in the conversation, an
   attached file, or ask the user to paste/point at one if genuinely missing.
   If the source lives in an attached file rather than the conversation, read
   it with the appropriate file-reading tool before rewriting.
2. **Extract the facts and structure** — every claim, technical detail, number,
   and section sequence from the source must survive the rewrite untouched. This
   skill changes voice, not substance.
3. **Weigh the draft first, then rewrite paragraph by paragraph**, applying the Voice
   Profile rules above. On anything over ~1,500 words, count the fragments, admissions,
   parentheticals and short aphoristic closers before you start, and apply rules 29-31
   only where the density is already high.
   Do not simply add a few colloquialisms to the existing draft — restructure
   sentences so the cadence itself changes (comma splices, pronoun slides,
   fragment afterthoughts).
4. **Check the opening and closing specifically** - these are where the voice is
   most distinct. The opening should ground the idea in something relatable
   before going abstract. For the closing, pick the mode per rule 18: wry and
   internal for a short post, practical advice for a long technical piece.
5. **Strip every em dash and semicolon** as a final pass before presenting. This is
   mechanical, do it last, and check the piece again after any later edit.
6. **Present the rewritten piece inline** as markdown by default. Only produce a
   file (docx) if the user explicitly asks for one.
7. **Do not fabricate** new facts, struggles, or specifics that aren't implied by
   the source material — candor and self-deprecation should come from real
   details already present (e.g. "this took a while" can become "longer than I
   will admit here," but don't invent a specific unrelated failure that never
   happened).

## Output
- Format: inline markdown, same overall structure/length as the source unless the
  user asks to shorten/lengthen
- Tone: conversational, candid, plainspoken — per the Voice Profile above
- Always preserve technical accuracy and factual content exactly

## Guardrails
- Never invent facts, incidents, or details not implied by the source draft.
- Always preserve every number, name, date, and technical detail from the
  source exactly as given.
- If the source is a formal, legal, or regulatory document, confirm with the
  user that this voice is appropriate before applying it — don't assume it is.
- If the source text can't be found, ask the user to paste it or point at the
  file rather than guessing at content.
- Show the rewritten piece to the user before it's delivered as an email or
  Teams message — never deliver it automatically.
- Don't over-apply every voice rule to every sentence. One or two markers per
  paragraph is usually enough, and cramming every rule into one passage reads as
  parody. Restraint is itself part of the voice.
- Never introduce an em dash or a semicolon into his text, including in headings,
  bullets, and parentheses. If the source had them, they go.
